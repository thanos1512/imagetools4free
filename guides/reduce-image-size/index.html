How to Reduce Image File Size Without Losing Quality

Updated October 24, 2026 · ImageTools4Free

Photos from a modern phone are often 3 to 8 MB each. That is far more than you need for most jobs. A big file is slow to upload, fills up storage, can be rejected by online forms, and makes websites load slowly. The good news is that you can usually cut the size by 80–90% without anyone noticing a difference.

This guide explains the three things that control file size, and the order to do them in.

What makes an image file big?

Three things decide how big an image file is:

  1. Dimensions: the number of pixels, written as width × height. A 4000 × 3000 photo has 12 million pixels. A 1200 × 900 version has just over 1 million, more than ten times fewer.
  2. Compression: how much detail the format is allowed to simplify to save space. JPEG and WebP let you choose a quality level; lower quality means a smaller file.
  3. Format: JPEG, PNG and WebP store pictures in different ways. The same photo can be several times bigger as a PNG than as a JPEG.

Most people only think about compression, but dimensions usually make the biggest difference.

Step 1: Crop away what you do not need

If part of the picture is not useful, such as empty sky or a messy background, remove it first with the Image Cropper. Fewer pixels means a smaller file, and the important part of the photo becomes clearer.

Step 2: Resize to the size you actually need

Think about where the image will be shown. A photo on a web page is rarely displayed wider than 1200–1600 pixels, even on a large screen. A profile picture may only be 400 pixels wide. Sending a 4000-pixel photo for those uses wastes space.

Open the Image Resizer, keep Keep proportions switched on, and type the new width. As a rough guide:

Where the image will be usedSuggested width
Full-width website banner1600–1920 px
Image inside a blog post or article1000–1200 px
Email attachment1200–1600 px
Thumbnail or profile photo300–500 px

Step 3: Compress

Now open the Image Compressor. Start with quality at 75% and press Compress. The tool shows the size before and after, so you can see what you saved. Zoom into the preview and look at areas with fine detail, such as hair, leaves or text. If it still looks good, try a lower setting. If you can see blocky patches or blurry edges, go back up.

For most photos, a quality between 65% and 80% is the sweet spot.

Step 4: Choose the right format

JPEG is the best all-round choice for photos you are sending or uploading somewhere, because every device and program can open it. WebP usually makes files about a quarter to a third smaller than JPEG at the same quality and is ideal for your own website. PNG is best for screenshots, logos and graphics with sharp edges or text, and for images that need a transparent background, but it is a poor choice for photos because the files are large. Read our JPG vs PNG vs WebP guide for more detail.

A real example

A typical phone photo might be 4032 × 3024 pixels and about 4 MB. After resizing it to 1600 pixels wide it might be around 2 MB as a PNG, and after compressing it as a JPEG at 75% quality it is often 200–350 KB. That is more than 90% smaller, and on a normal screen it looks the same. Exact numbers vary from photo to photo: busy scenes with lots of detail compress less than simple ones.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Compressing the same file again and again. Each JPEG save loses a little detail. Always start from your original photo.
  • Compressing before resizing. Resize first, then compress once at the end.
  • Using PNG for photos. A photo saved as PNG is often several times bigger than the same photo as JPEG.
  • Deleting the original. Keep the full-size version in case you need to print it or edit it later.

Summary

Crop, resize, then compress, in that order, and pick JPEG for sharing or WebP for websites. All of these tools run in your browser, so your photos stay on your device the whole time.
